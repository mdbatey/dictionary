# Korean Grammar Notes — Langfocus

Compiled from screenshots of the *Langfocus* YouTube channel's Korean grammar explainer (September 2026). Covers verb/adjective conjugation, particles, and sentence structure basics.

## Verbs and adjectives conjugate the same way

When an adjective sits in the predicative position (right after the subject), it functions like a verb and takes the same conjugation endings.

산이 높아요 (san-i nop-a-yo) — "The mountain is tall." (informal polite present) 산이 높았어요 (san-i nop-ass-eo-yo) — "The mountain was tall." (informal polite past)

Breakdown: adjective stem (높-) + tense affix (-아/-았) + polite affix (-요).

## Dictionary form and stems

Every verb and adjective's dictionary (citation) form ends in -다 (da).

말다 (mal-da) — "to stop" 자다 (ja-da) — "to sleep" 먹다 (meog-da) — "to eat"

Drop -다 to get the stem (말\~, 자\~, 먹\~), then attach the appropriate ending.

## Vowel-harmony rule for present-tense endings

- Stem's last vowel is ㅏ (a) or ㅗ (o) → add -아(요) \[-a(yo)\]
- Stem's last vowel is ㅓ (eo), ㅜ (u), ㅣ (i), or a combination vowel → add -어(요) \[-eo(yo)\]

Example: 먹다 "to eat" (stem ends in ㅓ) → 나는 떡볶이를 먹어요 (Na-neun tteog-bokk-i-leul meog-eo-yo) — "I eat tteokbokki."

## Past tense

The past tense equals the present-tense form with -sseo (았/었) inserted before the ending.

그는 먹다 말아요 → 그는 먹다 말았어요 (keu-neun meog-da mar-a-yo → mar-ass-eo-yo) — "He stops eating" → "He stopped eating." 그는 자요 → 그는 잤어요 (keu-neun ja-yo → jass-eo-yo) — "He sleeps" → "He slept."

## Future ("probable future")

- Consonant-ending stem → add -을 거예요 (eul keo-ye-yo)
- Vowel-ending stem → add -ㄹ 거예요 (\~l keo-ye-yo)

나는 떡볶이를 먹을 거예요 (Na-neun tteog-bokk-i-leul meog-eul keo-ye-yo) — "I'm going to eat tteokbokki" / "I shall eat tteokbokki."

## Irregular ㅡ-stem verbs

If a stem ends in ㅡ (eu), that vowel is replaced entirely with -어요 (eo-yo) rather than following the normal harmony rule.

애쓰다 (ae-sseu-da) "to try" → 그들은 애써요 (geu-deul-eun ae-sseo-yo) — "They try."

## Sentence-final rule

Every sentence in Korean must end with either a verb or an adjective. Verbs and adjectives behave similarly (both conjugate for past/non-past tense and speech level).

## Modifiers and word order

Modifiers — including full clauses — come before the noun they modify, and Korean has no relative pronouns.

내가 오르고 싶은 산 (nae-ga o-leu-go sip-eun san) — literally "An I-want-to-climb mountain," i.e. "A mountain I want to climb."

Korean also has no definite or indefinite articles (a/the).

## Particles

| Particle | Form | Function | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subject marker | 이 after consonant, 가 after vowel | Marks new or emphasized information (often answers an implicit "who/what") | 내가 야구를 해요 (nae-ga yagu-leul hae-yo) — "I \[specifically\] play baseball" |
| Object marker | 을 after consonant, 를 after vowel | Marks the direct object | 야구를 (yagu-leul), 떡볶이를 (tteog-bokk-i-leul) |
| Topic marker | 은 after consonant, 는 after vowel | Marks the topic/subject in a general statement | 나는 (na-neun) "as for me," 그는 (keu-neun) "as for him" |
| Direction/location | 에 | "to / into" a place | 방에 (bang-e) "into the room" |

## Basic sentence order: SOV

Korean is a verb-final language. A typical sentence is Subject–Object–Verb, unlike English's SVO.

나는 야구를 해요 (na-neun yagu-leul hae-yo) — "I play baseball." S = 나는, O = 야구를, V = 해요.

Particles attach after nouns/pronouns to mark their grammatical function or relationship to other words in the sentence.

## The copula 이다 and negative 아니다

이다 (ida) "to be" attaches directly to a noun to form the predicate:

학생이에요 (hag-saeng-i-e-yo) — "\[is\] a student." Breakdown: noun (학생) + subject-type marker (이) + 에요 (present polite ending).

Its negative counterpart is a separate verb, 아니다 (anida) "to not be," which is irregular — it's one of only a couple of verbs where the subject marker 이/가 attaches to the noun *before* the verb rather than the noun simply taking the copula:

그녀는 좋은 학생이 아니예요 (geu-nyeo-neun joh-eun hag-saeng-i a-ni-ye-yo) — "She is not a good student." Literally: she-topic / good-connector / student-subj / is-not.

Note 좋은 (joh-eun) — the adjective 좋다 "good" takes a connector (-은) and sits directly before the noun it modifies, per the modifier-before-noun rule.

## Hada verbs (noun + 하다)

Many Korean verbs are built from two parts: a Sino-Korean noun + 하다 (hada, "to do/make"). This is a very productive pattern for turning nouns into verbs.

일하다 (il ha-da) — "to work" 공부하다 (kong-bu ha-da) — "to study" 샤워하다 (sya-weo ha-da) — "to shower" 요리하다 (yo-li ha-da) — "to cook"

Example in past tense, informal non-polite: 네가 요리했어? (nae-ga yo-li-haess-eo) — "Did you cook \[it\]?" (요리 "cooking" + 했어, past tense of 하다).

## Existence and possession: 있다 / 없다

있다 (itda) "there is/are, to exist" and its negative 없다 (eopda) "there isn't/aren't, to not exist" are used to express possession ("have"/"don't have") — Korean has no separate verb "to have."

나는 자전거가 있어요. 하지만 차가 없어요. (na-neun ja-jeon-geo-ga iss-eo-yo. ha-ji-man cha-ga eops-eo-yo.) "I have a bicycle, but I don't have a car."

Literally: "As for me, a bicycle exists. But a car doesn't exist." The possessor takes the topic marker (나는), and the possessed item takes the subject marker (자전거가 / 차가).

Note the topic (나는) is dropped in the second clause since it's already clear from context — a topic only needs to be stated once it's established.

## Connecting clauses: 하지만 / -지만 ("but")

하지만 (ha-ji-man) can begin a new sentence to mean "but" / "however," linking it back to the previous sentence (as in the bicycle/car example above).

## Speech levels

Korean has four main speech levels, chosen based on who you're talking to:

| Level | Used with |
| --- | --- |
| Informal polite | Friends, acquaintances, colleagues of similar status, shop staff, etc. (the level covered throughout this doc so far — the -요/-아요/-어요 endings) |
| Informal non-polite | Very close friends, a brother/sister, someone a few years younger than you, or someone you want to be rude to |
| Formal polite | Someone of higher status than you, or people you're meeting for the first time |
| Formal non-polite | A neutral form used when you don't know who the reader/listener is (e.g. writing); also usable with friends of the same age |

Most of the earlier examples in this doc use the informal polite level by default, since that's what beginners typically learn first.

## Speech levels in practice: 말다 ("to stop") across all four

| Level | Form | Romanization |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Informal polite | 말아요 | mar-a-yo |
| Informal non-polite | 말아 | mar-a |
| Formal polite | 맙니다 | mam-ni-da |
| Formal non-polite | 만다 | man-da |

The informal non-polite form is simply the informal polite form with -요 dropped. The formal forms follow their own separate pattern (-ㅂ니다/-습니다 for polite, -는다/-ㄴ다 for non-polite) rather than being derived from the informal ones.
