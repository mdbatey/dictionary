# Langfocus Visual Teaching Style Guide

Notes on the visual/pedagogical style used in *Langfocus*'s Korean grammar slides, so it can be reused to teach other concepts. The core idea: color is a consistent functional legend, not decoration, layered with alignment and transformation arrows to make grammar traceable rather than just stated.

## 1. Functional color-coding

Each grammatical role gets its own consistent highlight color across a slide (and ideally across a whole series): subject marker, verb/adjective stem, tense affix, polite ending, object marker, etc. The same color always maps to the same function, so a viewer learns "yellow = subject marker" and can then scan new sentences by color alone rather than re-reading a rule each time.

**To reuse this:** pick a fixed palette before making any slide (e.g. yellow = subject, teal = stem/root, pink = tense, cyan = politeness/formality) and never reassign a color to a different function within the same series.

## 2. Three-tier interlinear stacking

Every example sentence stacks three aligned layers, top to bottom:

1. **Word-by-word English gloss** — small text directly above each Korean word/particle, a literal per-morpheme meaning (e.g. "mtn. / subj / tall / pres. / pol"), not a natural translation.
2. **Korean script** — large, bold, color-coded by grammatical function (see section 1).
3. **Romanization** — directly below, hyphenated at morpheme boundaries (not syllable boundaries), often color-matched to the segments above it.

All three layers share the same horizontal alignment, so each column reads top-to-bottom as one unit: meaning → script → sound.

The **natural, full-sentence English translation** is kept separate from this stack — placed to the side or below in quotation marks — so a literal gloss is never confused with an idiomatic translation.

## 3. Morpheme-splitting with arrows

For dense conjugations, the word is exploded into its component pieces with arrows pointing from each syllable/segment down to a labeled box naming what that piece does. Two layouts appear:

- **Linear breakdown** — arrows drop straight down from each syllable to a row of boxes underneath (stem → tense affix → polite affix), in reading order.
- **Fan-out breakdown** — one word sits at the top and arrows radiate outward/downward to labeled boxes spread horizontally, more like a small mind-map. Same information, laid out to spread across the slide rather than stack in a line.

Either way, the word is visibly deconstructed on-screen rather than the rule simply being stated in a sentence.

## 4. Before/after transformation arrows

When showing a change of state — most often present → past tense — the two full sentences are placed side by side connected by a red arrow. The changed portion is isolated in a colored box or in red text, so the *delta* (what actually changed) is the visual focus, not the whole sentence.

Example pattern: 그는 먹다 말아요 ("He stops eating") **→** 그는 먹다 말았어요 ("He stopped eating"), with the inserted -았- boxed in red and an arrow/label pointing to it reading "Indicates past."

## 5. Selective red highlighting (temporary focus layer)

Separate from the permanent function-color system (section 1), red is reserved as a live "this is what I'm talking about right now" marker, synced to narration. Examples: a red box drawn around just the past-tense insertion (-ass-eo-) to flag it mid-explanation, or a red arrow connecting a Korean phrase to its English translation.

This is a second, temporary emphasis layer laid on top of the permanent color legend — it changes from slide to slide depending on what's being discussed, while the function-colors stay fixed.

## 6. Bracket grouping for labels

A curly brace on the right side of the slide groups two or more related example lines under one shared label — e.g. multiple conjugation examples bracketed together under "Informal polite present tense." This clusters related patterns visually instead of repeating a caption under each example.

## 7. Formatting conventions

- Romanization is hyphenated at morpheme boundaries, not syllable boundaries (mar-a-yo, not ma-ra-yo) — this reinforces which chunk is the stem vs. the affix, tying back into the color-coding.
- The natural English translation sits to the right of (or below) the Korean sentence, in quotes, visually separated from the word-by-word gloss line above the romanization.
- In morpheme-breakdown slides (arrows-to-boxes layouts), the gloss line is sometimes dropped entirely since the labeled boxes are already doing that explanatory work.

## 8. Checklist for applying this style to new material

- [ ] Fix a color palette (one function per color) before building any slide
- [ ] For every example: gloss line above, Korean in the middle, romanization below — all aligned
- [ ] Keep the natural translation visually separate from the literal gloss
- [ ] When showing a transformation (tense, conjugation change), place before/after side by side with an arrow and isolate the changed piece
- [ ] Use red only as a temporary "look here now" marker, never as a permanent function color
- [ ] Group related examples under one bracketed label instead of repeating captions
- [ ] Hyphenate romanization at morpheme boundaries, not syllables

## 9. Phrase-boundary boxes drawn on the sentence itself

Beyond fill-color highlighting (section 1), he sometimes draws colored rectangle *outlines* directly around clusters of Korean text to mark phrase or clause boundaries — e.g. boxing "그녀는" (subject+topic) in red, "좋은" (adjective) in black, "학생이 아니예요" (predicate) in green, with each box's arrow pointing down to its own explanation. This is a second, independent visual layer on top of fill-color: **fill color = grammatical function of a word**, **box border = a phrase/clause grouping of several words**. The two layers can use unrelated colors from each other without conflicting, since they're answering different questions (what does this word do vs. what chunk does it belong to).

## 10. Back-to-back minimal-pair slides

For a contrast like affirmative vs. negative, he sometimes runs two nearly-identical slides in sequence (same layout, same colors, same box positions) where only the key word differs — e.g. "She is a good student" immediately followed by "She is not a good student," with 이에요 swapped for 아니예요 in the same visual slot. Because everything else on the slide is held constant, the viewer's eye goes straight to the one thing that changed, without needing a side-by-side split screen.

## 11. Rule box paired with a matching example list

When introducing a productive pattern (like noun + 하다 → verb), he places a plain-text box stating the formula ("A verb that consists of 2 parts: a noun + 하다") next to a short vertical list of 3-4 other words that follow the exact same pattern, each with its own romanization in red beneath it. The worked example in the main sentence proves the rule works once; the side list proves it generalizes — without redoing the full color/gloss/romanization breakdown for every item in the list (those get a lighter, quicker treatment: just Korean, translation, and romanization).

## 12. Split-panel layout with a divider

For content that splits into two registers or categories (informal vs. formal speech), a vertical divider line partitions the slide into two zones, sometimes with the right-hand zone additionally boxed as its own bordered panel. This groups formal-register examples visually apart from informal ones, so the split is legible even before reading any labels — the panel boundary itself communicates "these belong to a different category."

## 13. Color used inside English explanatory prose

On conceptual (non-example) slides — like the speech-levels overview — he sometimes colors individual English words *inside a descriptive sentence itself* (not just Korean text): the category name in bold red ("Informal polite") and the key audience-type nouns in blue within the explanation ("friends," "colleagues," "shop staff"). This extends the same color-as-signal principle from section 1 into pure prose, letting a viewer scan a paragraph for the load-bearing nouns rather than reading every word.

## 14. One word, four forms, stacked

When comparing a single word across all four speech levels, he stacks all four conjugated forms vertically in one place (말아요 / 말아 / 맙니다 / 만다) rather than spreading them across separate example sentences. This turns the abstract idea of "speech levels" into a very concrete, minimal visual: one root, four surface forms, seen together at a glance — the differences do the explaining instead of a paragraph of rules.
